Frameworks to keep handy
Product design: CIRCLES
Comprehend the situation, identify the customer, report their needs, cut through prioritization, list solutions, evaluate trade-offs, summarize your recommendation. Don't follow it blindly; use it so you never skip a step.
Metrics: AARM
Acquisition, activation, retention, monetization. Identify many metrics, prioritize one, diagnose with an issue tree, then propose three fixes and recommend one.
Estimation
Choose top-down or bottom-up, say every assumption aloud, calculate in steps, sanity-check, and name the assumption you'd validate first.
New markets
Market (size, growth, margins, trends), competitive environment, and company fit.
Prioritization: RICE
Reach x impact x confidence, divided by effort. Then check against strategy.
Behavioral: DIGS
Dramatize the situation, indicate the alternatives, go through what you did, summarize your impact.

Design and critique

Product design questions reward a clear customer, a prioritized need, and a single recommendation. Use CIRCLES as scaffolding, not a script.

1Do you like Gmail's Smart Compose?

Judge it on four criteria, then give a verdict and metrics.

Yes, overall I do, and let me explain how I'm judging it. I evaluate a product on four questions: does it solve a real problem, is it easy to understand, is it unobtrusive, and does it respect the user?

On the first three it does well. Writing routine emails is repetitive, and Smart Compose removes that friction with a suggestion that appears inline. The user accepts it with one keystroke or ignores it by simply typing on, so the cost of a bad suggestion is almost zero.

My concerns are about the fourth question. Suggestions can nudge everyone toward the same generic phrasing, and they can be subtly wrong in a way you miss when you're skimming. Some people also worry about what a model learns from their writing.

So my verdict is that I like it for routine email. I'd improve it with lightweight tone controls and a clear privacy explanation. To know whether it's working, I'd track accept rate and characters saved, and I'd also track how much people edit after accepting, because edits tell me about quality, not just usage.

2Tell me about a product you think is poorly designed.

Pick one product, name the user and goal, show specific failures, propose a fix, and say how you'd measure it.

I'd pick airport self check-in kiosks, because I can describe the failure specifically.

The user is a traveler who wants to check in and drop a bag in under two minutes. The kiosk gets in their way in three ways. The first screen offers several options with equal weight, so people hesitate. Upsells like seat upgrades interrupt the core task before the bag tag is printed. And when something goes wrong, the error message tells you what failed but not what to do next, so people end up waiting for a staff member.

The result is long queues, and staff spend their time overriding the machine instead of helping people who really need help.

I'd fix it with one primary action per screen, upsells moved to after the task is complete, error messages that name the next step, and a help button that's always visible. I'd measure success by the share of passengers who finish without staff help and by the average time to complete.

9Design a way for advertisers to see whether their ads truly drive sales.

Frame correlation versus incrementality, customer, three solutions, recommendation, limits, metrics.

The key distinction is between correlation and incrementality. Reported conversions tell you who bought after seeing an ad. Incrementality tells you who bought because of the ad, and that's the question a skeptical CFO cares about.

So I'll design for a mid-size retailer with online and offline sales and a CFO who doesn't trust attribution reports.

I see three solutions. First, in-product lift experiments, such as geo tests or holdouts. Second, modeled attribution with marketing-mix style reporting. Third, a plain-language summary that shows estimated incremental sales using both.

I'd recommend guided lift tests with the plain-language summary on top, because a result the CFO can understand and trust is what actually changes budget decisions. Small advertisers may not have enough volume for clean holdouts, so for them I'd lean on modeling.

I'd measure the share of advertisers running a test, how often budgets change after results, and trust scores.

23Gmail daily active users dropped 5% week over week. Diagnose.

Is it real? Issue tree, segment, eliminate, fix by cause.

My first question is whether the drop is real. I'd check for logging changes or changes in how active users are defined.

If it's real, I'd organize the possible causes into an issue tree. On the product side: a recent release, an outage, a sign-in problem, or a spam wave. On the user side: seasonality, holidays, or a mismatch in days of the week. External causes could include a competitor, a policy change, or network blocks. And there could be a mix shift, for example more traffic from a region or platform with lower usage.

To narrow it down, I'd compare the same weekday, then segment by platform, country, and new versus existing users, and line the drop up against release timing. I'd rank the hypotheses by how likely they are and how cheap they are to check, then eliminate them one by one.

Once I know the cause, the fix follows: roll back a bad release, repair a sign-in issue, or, if it's seasonal, simply document it and keep watching.

24Reported conversions for EU advertisers dropped 10% overnight. What do you do?

Triage reality versus reporting, hypotheses, segment, act, monitor.

First, I'd separate reality from reporting. A sudden overnight drop often means something changed in the pipeline, so I'd check for logging incidents and dashboard issues.

If the data is real, I have four hypotheses. Consent: a change in a consent tool or in the consent signals could have increased the number of denied states. Modeling: some advertisers may have fallen below the volume needed for modeled conversions. Tags: a large advertiser may have deployed a change that broke their tag. And external factors like a browser release or a holiday.

I'd segment by country, consent tool, tag type, and advertiser size. If the drop is concentrated in one consent tool, that points to consent. If it's concentrated in a few advertisers, it's likely a tag issue.

Then I'd act: communicate proactively to affected advertisers, fix what's on our side, and advise them on what's on theirs. Finally, I'd add monitoring on consent rates, the modeled share of conversions, and tag health so we catch it sooner next time.

25In an A/B test, click-through rate and ad revenue rose, but user satisfaction fell. Do you ship?

Ask the objective, weigh short versus long term, recommend, guardrail.

First I'd ask what the current company objective is, because this is a classic win-lose trade-off and the right answer depends on the priority.

Revenue and clicks going up while satisfaction falls could mean the change is just more intrusive. The danger is that satisfaction is a leading indicator of retention, so the short-term revenue gain may reverse later.

Before deciding, I'd check the size of each effect, which user segments drive it, and whether satisfaction has historically predicted retention.

My recommendation is not to launch broadly. I'd iterate on the design, for example fewer or better-targeted ads, and run a long-term holdback group to measure the effect on retention. I treat user trust as a guardrail that shouldn't be traded away, unless the company's stated objective clearly says otherwise.

26An experiment shows +2% conversions with p = 0.09. Ship?

Interpret p, check design and cost of being wrong, recommend.

A p-value of 0.09 isn't below the usual 0.05 threshold, but it also doesn't mean there's no effect. It means that if there were no real effect, we'd see a result this large about nine percent of the time.

To decide, I'd consider a few things. Did we agree on a threshold beforehand? Did the test reach its planned sample size? What does the confidence interval look like, and does it exclude harmful outcomes? Are there novelty effects, and did any guardrail metrics move? And how reversible is the change?

My recommendation is to extend the test to its planned sample rather than peeking and stopping on a good-looking number. If the change is low-risk and easy to reverse, a monitored rollout is defensible. If it's expensive or hard to undo, I'd wait for stronger evidence.

35Your A/B test has a sample ratio mismatch (52/48 when 50/50 was planned). What do you do?

Recognize the bug, confirm, investigate, don't ship, communicate.

A sample ratio mismatch is a red flag that something is wrong with the randomization or the logging, which means the results can't be trusted, so I would not ship.

First I'd confirm the mismatch is bigger than chance would explain, using a chi-square test. Then I'd investigate: the assignment logic, bot traffic, differences in logging loss between the two groups, and how users enter the experiment.

Once I find the cause, I'd fix it and re-run the test. And I'd tell stakeholders clearly that the earlier result is invalid and why, so nobody makes a decision based on it.

36How would you measure the impact of ads on brand awareness when no purchase happens?

Options, trade-offs, recommendation, metrics.

Without a purchase to count, I'd use several approaches together. Brand lift surveys compare people who saw the ads with a control group. I could also look at lift in brand-term searches, changes in direct traffic, geo-holdout experiments, and marketing-mix modeling for longer time horizons.

Each has trade-offs. Surveys can be biased, experiments cost money, and small samples are noisy.

My recommendation is a brand lift study paired with a geo experiment, so the survey tells me about attitudes and the geo test tells me about behavior. The metrics would be ad recall, consideration, and brand search lift.

37An advertiser's match rate is 85%, yet attributed conversions barely rose after uploading first-party data. Why?

Match rate is not incrementality, hypotheses, checks, insight.

The key insight is that match rate measures how many records matched, not whether they added anything new.

There are several possible explanations. Most of those conversions may already have been counted through other tracking, so the upload mostly duplicates them. There may have been few offline conversions to add in the first place. The data may arrive after the conversion window closes. The conversion action may be misconfigured, so bidding doesn't use it. Or consent settings may limit how much can be used.

I'd check which uploaded conversions are new versus duplicates, how much delay there is in uploads, and the conversion-action settings.

The broader lesson is that success should be defined as incremental measured conversions and better bidding performance, not a high match rate on its own.

38How would you explain correlation versus causation in ad measurement to a CMO?

64You have a sessions table (session_id, channel) and an orders table (order_id, session_id, revenue). How would you compute conversion rate and revenue by channel?

I'd start from the sessions table and use a left join to orders. That way, sessions without an order still count in the denominator. Then I'd group by channel and use distinct counts, so a session with several orders isn't counted as multiple conversions.

Here's the query:

SELECT s.channel,
       COUNT(DISTINCT s.session_id) AS sessions,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.session_id) AS converting_sessions,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.session_id) * 1.0
         / COUNT(DISTINCT s.session_id) AS conversion_rate,
       COALESCE(SUM(o.revenue), 0) AS revenue
FROM sessions s
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.session_id = s.session_id
GROUP BY s.channel;

A few caveats. An inner join would drop non-converting sessions and inflate the conversion rate. Revenue is safe to sum here because each order appears once. And real attribution, with windows and multiple touchpoints, would need more logic than this.

113Design an AI-powered chatbot that helps advertisers troubleshoot campaign issues.

114How would you measure whether an AI feature is actually good, not just heavily used?

Separate usage from quality, define quality signals, add human review, set hard guardrails.

Usage alone can be misleading. People might use a feature a lot because it's the default, because it's novel, or because they're stuck in a loop trying to get a better answer, which actually signals poor quality.

So I'd separate two things: adoption, which tells me people are trying it, and quality, which tells me it's actually helping. For quality, I'd look at explicit signals like thumbs up and down, implicit signals like edit rate and how often people abandon a generated result without using it, and task success, meaning whether the person accomplished what they came to do.

I'd supplement automated signals with a regular human review of a sample of outputs, since automated proxies can be gamed or miss subtle errors that matter, like a factually wrong but confident-sounding answer.

For guardrails, I'd track harmful or clearly wrong outputs as a hard limit, separate from the quality score, so a rise in average quality can never hide a rise in serious failures.

115A generative AI feature increases engagement significantly but costs ten times more per interaction to serve. How do you decide whether to keep it?

116How would you evaluate whether to use retrieval-augmented generation or fine-tuning for an AI support feature?

Define both, decision criteria, recommendation, hybrid option.

With retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, the model is given relevant documents at the time of the question and answers based on them. With fine-tuning, the model's own weights are adjusted using examples, so the knowledge is baked in.

I'd choose based on a few things. How often does the underlying information change? If it changes often, like pricing or policy, RAG is easier to keep current, since you just update the documents. How much do you need the model to follow a very specific style or reasoning pattern? That favors fine-tuning. And how much proprietary or sensitive data is involved, since RAG keeps that data out of the model weights, which can matter for privacy and the ability to remove something later.

For a support feature grounded in policies that change often, I'd default to RAG, since freshness and the ability to cite a source both matter for trust.

In practice, I'd often combine both: RAG for facts that change, with light fine-tuning or careful prompting for tone and format, and I'd validate the choice with an evaluation set of real support questions before committing.

117How would you design the experience for when an AI feature gets something wrong?

I'd start from the assumption that the AI will sometimes be wrong, and design the experience around graceful recovery rather than pretending it won't happen.

First, I'd make it easy to tell when something might be uncertain, for example showing a confidence cue or citing sources so the user can judge for themselves rather than trusting blindly. Second, I'd make correction effortless: an easy way to edit, regenerate, or fall back to a manual path without feeling like they broke something. Third, I'd make feedback nearly free to give, like a single tap, so we can learn from failures at scale.

I'd avoid two failure modes: overconfident phrasing that makes wrong answers sound authoritative, and a dead end where the only option after a bad answer is to give up.

I'd measure the recovery rate, meaning how often a user successfully gets what they need after an initial bad response, alongside the raw error rate, since a system that fails occasionally but recovers gracefully can still be a good experience.

I'd build this bottom-up. Of 50 million monthly users, I'll assume 10 percent try the AI assistant in a given month, so 5 million users. Of those, I'll assume an average of 5 queries per user per month, so 25 million queries a month.

Next, cost per query. I'll assume an average query, including the context provided to the model, costs about a cent to serve. That gives 25 million times $0.01, which is about $250,000 a month.

I'd sanity-check this against the value it's expected to create, for example retention or engagement lift, to see if the cost is justified, and I'd flag that cost per query varies a lot by task complexity, so I'd break it into simple and complex query types rather than using one blended average once we have real data.

The assumptions I'd validate first are the adoption rate and the cost per query, since together they drive almost the entire estimate, and both are usually wrong in the first draft by a wide margin.

120Should every product feature eventually be reimagined around AI, or are there features that should stay deliberately simple and non-AI?

Take a position, the case for AI-everywhere, the case against, recommendation, a test.

My position is no, not every feature should be rebuilt around AI, and reaching for it by default is a mistake.

The case for AI is real: it can handle ambiguity, personalize, and automate work that used to require a person. But the case against matters just as much: AI adds cost, latency, and unpredictability, and for tasks that are simple, high-stakes, or need to be perfectly consistent, a deterministic system is often better and cheaper.

My recommendation is to apply a simple test before adding AI to anything: is the task ambiguous or highly variable, is some rate of imperfect results acceptable, and does the value of personalization or automation clearly exceed the added cost and risk? If the answer to any of those is no, I'd keep it simple.

A good example is a login flow or a billing calculation: those should stay simple, predictable, and boring, precisely because reliability matters more than flexibility there. AI earns its place where judgment and variability are the actual problem, not everywhere by default.

Frameworks to study

The quick-reference box up top is for recall in the room. This is the study list: sixteen frameworks worth actually understanding beforehand, why interviewers test them, and the mistake most candidates make with each. This section isn't affected by the category filters above; it's always here.

1CIRCLES (product design)

The go-to structure for "design a product for X" questions.

CIRCLES stands for comprehend the situation, identify the customer, report their needs, cut through prioritization, list solutions, evaluate trade-offs, and summarize your recommendation. It comes from the book Decode and Conquer, and interviewers use design questions specifically to see whether you naturally structure an open-ended problem instead of jumping straight to a solution.

Study it by practicing the sequence out loud until it feels automatic, then deliberately vary the pacing, since spending too long clarifying or too long brainstorming both read as weaknesses. The most common failure isn't forgetting a step, it's rushing to solutions before the customer and their need are clear.

2AARM (metrics diagnosis)

Use it to brainstorm broadly, then commit to one metric.

AARM organizes metrics around the user journey: acquisition, activation, retention, and monetization. It's a brainstorming tool, not the final answer, so the real skill it tests is whether you can generate several reasonable metrics and then defend picking just one as a north star.

For diagnosis questions, like "a metric dropped, why," pair AARM with an issue tree: is the drop real, what are the possible causes, and how would you segment the data to narrow it down. Interviewers are listening for structured elimination, not a lucky guess at the cause.

3Estimation approach (top-down and bottom-up)

State every assumption out loud, even the obvious ones.

Estimation questions test comfort with ambiguity and basic quantitative reasoning, not your ability to recall real numbers. Decide whether to go top-down, starting from a large population and narrowing down, or bottom-up, building from a small unit and scaling up, and say which one you're choosing and why.

Round numbers aggressively to keep the math clean, state each assumption as you use it, and finish with a sanity check against something you actually know, like store counts or population figures. Always name the one or two assumptions that would move the answer the most, since that shows judgment about what matters.

4New markets checklist (market, competition, fit)

For "should company X enter market Y" questions.

This checklist evaluates three things: the market itself, meaning its size, growth, margins, and trends, the competitive environment, meaning who's already there and how defensible their position is, and company fit, meaning whether the company's existing strengths and assets actually help here.

The most common mistake is skipping company fit and only evaluating whether the market is attractive in the abstract. A market can be huge and growing and still be a bad idea for a specific company if it doesn't build on anything that company is already good at.

5RICE (prioritization)

Score, then check the score against strategy.

RICE scores an initiative as reach times impact times confidence, divided by effort. It gives prioritization questions a quantitative backbone instead of a purely qualitative debate.

The part candidates most often skip is the step after scoring: checking the highest-scoring option against strategic goals, dependencies, and risk. A framework score is an input to the decision, not the decision itself, and saying that explicitly is what separates a mechanical answer from a judgment-driven one.

6DIGS (behavioral)

Dramatize, indicate alternatives, go through what you did, summarize impact.

DIGS is a structure for behavioral answers: dramatize the situation so the stakes are clear, indicate the alternatives you considered so the interviewer sees you weren't on autopilot, go through what you actually did, and summarize the impact with a number if you can.

It's close to the more widely known STAR method, situation, task, action, result, with one addition: explicitly naming alternatives you didn't take. That's the part worth rehearsing, since it's what shows judgment rather than just narrating events in order.

7HEART (UX quality metrics)

Google's own framework for measuring user experience quality.

HEART measures happiness, engagement, adoption, retention, and task success. It was developed at Google specifically to give UX and product teams a shared vocabulary for quality metrics beyond raw usage counts, so it's worth knowing by name in a Google interview.

Use it when a question asks you to evaluate the quality of an experience, not just whether people use it. Task success in particular is the metric most candidates forget: it measures whether people actually accomplished what they came to do, which usage numbers alone can't tell you.

8North Star Metric

The one number a whole team rallies around.

A north star metric is the single measure a team optimizes for because it best captures the value the product delivers to users, in a way that's expected to also drive business results if it improves.

A good north star reflects real value delivered, not just activity, is measurable in the near term, and is something the team can actually influence. When asked for "the one metric that matters," interviewers want to see you reject vanity metrics like raw signups or pageviews in favor of something that ties usage to value.

9Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)

Reframe the question around the job the customer is "hiring" the product to do.

Jobs to Be Done reframes a product around the underlying job a customer is trying to get done, rather than the product category itself. The classic example is that people don't want a drill, they want a hole in the wall, and really they want the shelf that hole makes possible.

It's useful for design and strategy questions where the obvious framing is too narrow. Naming the real job a customer is hiring the product for often reveals competitors you wouldn't otherwise consider, since anything that gets the same job done is a competitor, even if it looks nothing like your product.

10Kano Model

Sort features into basic, performance, and delight.

The Kano model classifies features into three types: basic features that cause dissatisfaction if missing but don't create much delight when present, performance features where more is simply better, and delight features that create disproportionate satisfaction because they're unexpected.

It's a good addition to a prioritization answer because it adds a dimension RICE doesn't capture well: not all high-impact features behave the same way as you invest more in them. Knowing which category a feature falls into changes whether it's worth over-investing in it.

11SWOT analysis

Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats.

SWOT separates internal factors, strengths and weaknesses, from external factors, opportunities and threats. It's a simple structure for strategy questions that ask you to evaluate a company's or product's position before recommending a direction.

Used well, it's a quick scan, not the whole answer: the real value comes from what you recommend after the scan, connecting a specific strength to a specific opportunity, rather than just listing four boxes of observations and stopping there.

12OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)

How companies like Google translate strategy into measurable goals.

An objective is a qualitative, ambitious statement of what you want to achieve. Key results are the specific, measurable outcomes that tell you whether you got there. Good key results are outcomes, not tasks, meaning "increase task success rate to 90 percent," not "ship feature X."

This is worth knowing cold for a Google interview specifically, since OKRs are how the company sets and communicates goals internally. If a question asks how you'd set goals for a team or a quarter, framing your answer as an objective with two or three measurable key results shows fluency with how the company actually operates.

13AARRR, or Pirate Metrics

A funnel-shaped cousin of AARM: acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue.

AARRR walks through the same early stages as AARM but adds referral as its own stage and treats the whole thing as a strict funnel: acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue. It's useful when a question is specifically about growth, since it forces you to consider how existing users bring in new ones, which AARM doesn't call out separately.

Knowing both frameworks and the difference between them is more useful than memorizing either one alone: AARM is a broader lens for organizing any product's metrics, while AARRR is sharper for questions specifically about a growth funnel.

14Impact/Effort Matrix

A fast, visual way to sort ideas into four quadrants.

This plots initiatives on two axes, impact and effort, creating four quadrants: quick wins, which are high impact and low effort, big bets, which are high impact and high effort, fill-ins, which are low impact and low effort, and time sinks, which are low impact and high effort.

It's a faster, more visual alternative to RICE when a question just wants a quick prioritization pass across many small ideas rather than a precise ranking of a few. Naming which quadrant something falls into is a quick way to justify deprioritizing an idea without a lot of math.

15Five Whys

Ask "why" repeatedly to get from a symptom to a root cause.

Five Whys is a simple root-cause technique: state the problem, ask why it happened, then ask why that answer happened, repeating roughly five times until you reach a cause that's actually actionable, rather than stopping at the first, most visible explanation.

It's a good tool to name explicitly in diagnostic questions, like a metric that dropped, because it signals that you won't stop investigating at the first plausible-sounding explanation. The discipline of it is refusing to recommend a fix until you've pushed past the surface-level symptom.

16Working Backwards and the PR-FAQ

Write the press release before you build the product.

Working backwards starts by writing the press release and FAQ for a product as if it already shipped, describing the customer benefit in plain language before any design or engineering work happens. If the press release doesn't sound compelling on its own, that's a signal the idea isn't strong enough yet.

Even outside companies that use this formally, it's a useful mental model for vision and product-design questions: if you can't describe the customer benefit in two plain sentences a real person would care about, the idea needs more work before you pitch a roadmap around it.
